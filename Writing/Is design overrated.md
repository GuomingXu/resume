# Is design overrated?

After visiting China, DHH shared [his reflections](https://x.com/dhh/status/2111795187454296232?s=20) on Chinese software. He was impressed by the country's progress in hardware and AI, but asked why so many of its digital products still feel cluttered and poorly crafted.

Some people blamed a lack of design appreciation. Others pointed to competition, organizational structures, and consumer preferences.

Having spent most of my career designing complex digital products, I agree with some of his observations. But I'm not convinced the problem is simply a lack of good design. The more interesting question is why these products became the way they are, and whether better design would really change their success.

# Design doesn't exist in isolation

WeChat, Alipay, Taobao, and Meituan are dense, sometimes overwhelmingly so. Promotions compete with features, banners compete with navigation, and almost every space serves a commercial purpose.

It's easy to call this bad design. But China's digital economy grew at extraordinary speed, and products were expected to do more, expand faster, and capture as much attention and spending as possible. Super apps were a natural result: ecosystems built to keep users inside.

Organizational incentives reinforce this. Product, marketing, and business teams each have their own goals. Every new feature or promotional entry point can be justified by a measurable outcome. The cumulative cost to the overall experience is much harder to measure.

I've heard the same argument many times: *Our competitors already do it this way. Why test it again?* Sometimes that's a fair shortcut. But when shipping is the top priority, design loses its ability to challenge assumptions. Designers stop deciding what should exist and start accommodating what has already been decided. That isn't a failure of designers. It's the predictable result of how organizations define success.

# Culture shapes what users expect

Aesthetic preferences don't develop independently of economic conditions or the products people use every day.

Chinese consumers have spent years navigating information-heavy screens, coupons, membership programs, and complicated pricing. They've become very good at scanning busy interfaces. When prices require careful comparison and people feel they need to verify claims before buying, more information can feel more useful. What looks like clutter to one person may be useful information to another.

But familiarity isn't the same as preference. Businesses add more promotions, interfaces get more complex, users learn to cope, and businesses read that behavior as proof the design works. The system reinforces itself.

**Do users genuinely prefer dense interfaces, or have they simply learned to navigate them well?** I suspect it's both.

# The Japanese paradox

Dense interfaces aren't unique to China. Rakuten and Yahoo! Shopping in Japan rely just as heavily on text, banners, and detailed product information.

Yet Japan is also known for the opposite: MUJI, its architecture, and the intentional use of empty space. How does a culture that values restraint produce such crowded shopping experiences?
Part of the answer is history. Japanese e-commerce carries over early web conventions, established merchant practices, and decades of user expectations, and changing something that still makes money is risky. Japanese visual culture has also never been only minimalist; it includes elaborate packaging, colorful signage, and manga.

There may be no universal link between a society's aesthetic traditions and its digital products. Culture matters, but so do technology, business models, organizational habits, and history.

# **Good UX is not necessarily a competitive moat**

The design industry has spent years telling us that good design creates business value. I don't disagree that it does. But I think we've overstated what design alone can achieve.

Good UX can reduce friction, improve efficiency, and increase conversion. None of that automatically creates a lasting advantage. A successful interaction pattern is easy to copy, without the research that produced it. And users often don't choose the product with the best experience. They choose the cheaper one, the one with more merchants, the one their friends use, or the one already built into their daily lives.

Network effects, distribution, pricing, supply chains, and switching costs are usually stronger advantages than interface quality.

**Good UX creates value. But value is not necessarily a moat.**

# Sometimes, design is a plus one

This may be uncomfortable for designers: a product doesn't always need an exceptional experience to succeed.

In B2B software, it's obvious. Many enterprise systems are hard to learn, outdated, and frustrating. Companies still pay for them because they run essential processes, integrate with existing infrastructure, and solve problems alternatives can't. The people using them aren't the people buying them.

In consumer products, where switching is easy, experience matters more. Even so, meeting the industry standard may be enough. Once a product is reasonably usable, extra polish often adds less value, especially when price or convenience drives the decision.

This doesn't mean usability is unimportant. A product that can't complete its core tasks has a fundamental experience problem, and no visual polish can fix that.

Sometimes design is a differentiator. Sometimes it's a necessity. Sometimes it's simply a plus one. The hard part is knowing which situation you're in.

# Have we overstated the value of design?

I think the design industry sometimes blends three different claims:

- Design can improve a product.
- Design can contribute to business success.
- Design can be the main reason a business succeeds.

These are not the same. Even design-led companies depend on technology, timing, execution, distribution, and a viable business model. It's hard to isolate design as the deciding factor.

With AI making it easier to generate interfaces and reproduce familiar patterns, visual and interaction quality alone will become even less defensible. The value of design may increasingly lie elsewhere: understanding complex problems, making trade-offs, shaping coherent systems, and deciding what's worth building at all. Those capabilities still have to prove their value within the realities of a business.

Designers shouldn't stop advocating for better experiences. But we should be more precise about when, why, and how they matter.

# What are we really designing for?

I understand DHH's disappointment. A country that builds sophisticated hardware and AI clearly has the talent to build beautifully crafted software.

But having the capability doesn't mean the market rewards it. If a crowded interface makes money, users have adapted to it, and competitors do the same, what reason does a company have to choose differently? Sometimes it's competition, a founder's commitment to craft, or a business model that benefits from simplicity. Sometimes there's no commercial reason at all.

That doesn't make the result good. It makes it understandable.

> **Good design matters. But perhaps not as much, or in the ways, the design industry has taught us to believe.**
> 

Design is shaped by culture, economics, competition, incentives, and learned behavior, and the products we make in turn shape what people accept. As designers, we should keep questioning unnecessary complexity. We should also be willing to question our own assumptions about why design matters.

Not every successful product is beautifully designed. Not every beautifully designed product succeeds. Understanding why may be as important as knowing how to design one.
