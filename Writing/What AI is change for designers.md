# What AI is change for designers

# Background

AI is the hottest topic in the industry right now. Our company has been actively encouraging everyone to explore its capabilities, our design community has unlocked Figma Make for everyone, and online, the conversation never stops. Every day, there are claims that AI will replace designers, replace developers, and eventually replace everyone.

I don't think that framing is the most useful way to understand what's actually happening.

What I observe instead is this: AI is changing the shape of work. It is changing how quickly we can produce drafts, how many directions we can explore, and how we communicate ideas. But it is not replacing the need for judgment, method, or collaboration.

That distinction became much clearer to me through a few real examples.

# Design foundation still matter

In one case, I asked a designer to put together a user research plan for a new project - to identify pain points from our end users and clarify new business needs. What came back looked polished at first glance. The structure appeared solid, the language sounded professional, and there was even a Triple Diamond diagram attached.

But the plan wasn't truly actionable. There was no clear "why," and no logical thread connecting the steps.

When I asked how it had been developed, the answer was simple: he hadn't been discussed with anyone - only with AI.

That moment stayed with me. The problem wasn't that AI had been used. I actually believe working with AI can be genuinely valuable. The deeper issue was that the person using it didn't understand user research deeply enough to evaluate the output. They didn't have a clear grasp of methodology, didn't know which methods fit which stage, and hadn't involved the team in shaping the direction. Without that foundation, AI didn't create clarity, it only created the appearance of clarity.

This is one of the most important lessons I've taken from the past year: **if you don't understand the what and the why, you can't effectively use AI to support the how.**

# **AI hallucination in practice**

I saw something similar play out in design tooling.

Earlier this year, when our company enabled Figma Make for everyone, we were all excited. My team started exploring it for solution exploration and prototyping, and we had high expectations - faster options, quicker prototypes, more efficient communication with stakeholders and engineers.

And to be fair, Figma Make did help in some ways. It was genuinely useful for generating different directions quickly, for expansion and early ideation.

But the gap between generation and delivery was much larger than we expected. Some outputs couldn't be easily reused in the actual design workflow. Some were difficult to compare side by side. Some prototypes looked promising at first, but couldn't accurately reproduce the intended interaction, even after repeated revisions. The tools were good at producing possibilities, but not always good at producing usable outcomes.

That forced me to ask a harder question: does AI actually improve efficiency, or does it sometimes just create the feeling of progress?

I think that's the wrong question to ask in the abstract. The better question is: **in which kinds of tasks does AI create real value?**

# **Where AI genuinely helps**

Because I've also had the opposite experience.

Recently, I worked on a set of email templates - twelve in total. The bulk of the work wasn't visual design; it was organizing the messaging logic and refining the copy. In that context, AI was genuinely useful. It helped me structure the communication, improve the writing, and move efficiently through a task that was time-consuming but well-defined.

That experience helped me see the difference more clearly.

AI tends to be most valuable when the task has a clear goal, when quality can be assessed quickly, and when the cost of correction is relatively low. It is far less reliable when the task depends on deep context, strong methodology, cross-team alignment, or consistency across a larger system.

# **Design thinking is the differentiator**

AI is not design thinking. It is not product judgment. It is not team collaboration. And it is definitely not a substitute for understanding *why* something should be designed in a particular way.

If anything, AI has made foundational design thinking more important, not less.

The tools will keep evolving. New products will appear, existing ones will improve, and workflows will keep shifting. But the fundamentals don't change at the same pace. Understanding design thinking, methodology, and human context still matters deeply - in fact, it matters more, because those are precisely what allow us to direct AI well and evaluate its output critically.

# Reflection

Think of AI as an amplifier.

If you know what you're doing, AI can amplify your speed, your range, and your leverage. If you don't, it will amplify vagueness, weak reasoning, and shallow output.

This also means not everything needs AI. Sometimes the fastest path is still the simplest one. Sometimes using AI introduces more setup, more correction, and more uncertainty than the task actually deserves. Sometimes a tool is interesting without being truly valuable.

I don't think designers need to chase every new AI tool the moment it appears. What matters more is understanding what a tool is genuinely good at, where it breaks down, and whether it meaningfully fits your workflow.

For me, that's the real mindset shift.

The question is no longer *"Can AI do this?"*

The better questions are: What part of this work is generation? What part is judgment? What part is collaboration? What part is still deeply human?

That's how I want to keep thinking about AI as a designer - not with panic, and not with blind optimism, but with clarity.
